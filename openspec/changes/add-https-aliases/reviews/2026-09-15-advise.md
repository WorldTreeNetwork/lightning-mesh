## Independent take (blind)

1. Pin one canonical public-key byte encoding, hash/truncation rule, DNS-label alphabet, case, and padding rule so every implementation derives the same key-qualified name.
2. Pin the complete app and router hostname grammar, including mesh-label validation and the configured-zone terminator, so suffix checks cannot drift into parent-zone handling.
3. Require dnsmasq forwarding and rebind exemption for the exact configured delegated zone only; refuse an exception for `worldtree.network` or any broader parent.
4. Specify the offline authoritative AliasTable inputs, persistence/refresh behavior, A/AAAA and HTTPS/SVCB answer scope, TTLs, and negative answers rather than relying on public DNS.
5. Require issuance authorization to prove possession of the public key encoded in the requested origin and to bind the CSR/public TLS key without letting an operator mint another owner’s name.
6. Make origin non-reuse durable across removal, reinstall, mesh-label changes, and state loss; an old browser origin must never become a different owner’s authority.
7. Keep every leaf TLS private key on its serving host and refuse shared leaf keys, wildcard certificates, wildcard aliases, or a router-wide certificate that collapses app ownership.
8. Preserve `http://hello.mesh` and its port-80 captive/front-desk behavior unchanged while adding a separately testable TLS listener and certificate-selection path.
9. Name the WTN adapter’s runner, trust boundary, input/output contract, failure behavior, and best-effort semantics so issuance cannot silently become a mandatory online dependency.
10. Tradeoff: an 80-bit truncated key label is operationally compact but accepts a bounded collision risk, so collision detection and fail-closed handling must accompany the usability win.

## Steelman and comparison

The design substantially answers the blind concerns. It pins the 80-bit BLAKE3/RFC 4648 lowercase alphabet, the two hostname forms, exact per-mesh dnsmasq suffixes, A-only AliasTable scope for this phase, owner authorization fields, host-local leaf keys, unchanged walk-up HTTP, `tiny_http` plus rustls/ring with an aarch64 measurement, and WTN as the best-effort default adapter. It also explicitly rejects shared/wildcard keys and parent-zone forwarding. Those are coherent choices: short opaque labels reduce CT disclosure and operational friction, while key-qualified names prevent routine owner turnover from inheriting browser state.

## Blocking findings

1. **The change disagrees with itself about who owns the AliasTable record contract.** `proposal.md:100` puts the owner-signed resolution-record format in a later sibling after `ai0.9`, while `design.md:135-139`, `tasks.md`, and the delta require those resolution records in this change. The AliasTable cannot be implemented or security-reviewed from “verified owner-signed records” alone: this change must own a versioned, domain-separated canonical signed payload (full owner key, exact FQDN, address value/scope, freshness or replacement semantics, and signature), plus deterministic conflict/removal behavior. Reconcile the proposal and make that contract explicit; `ai0.9` may supply primitives but cannot remain the owner of this record type.

2. **The owner key and leaf certificate key are conflated in the normative text.** The design correctly has the owner key authorize a CSR whose SPKI is host-local (`design.md:61-89`), but the delta says no hostname may be “served by a key other than the one its label derives from” (`spec.md:10`). A worker could read that as requiring the owner identity key itself to terminate TLS. Rewrite the requirement to distinguish (a) the owner/node authorization key encoded by the label from (b) the independently generated host-local TLS key bound by `csr_spki_sha256`, and add a wrong-CSR-key negative scenario.

3. **The promised non-recycling invariant is stronger than the truncated-label design.** The delta requires every K1→K2 owner change to yield L1≠L2 (`spec.md:9,14-18`), but the label is only an 80-bit prefix and the design provides no collision rule. Keep the accepted 80-bit tradeoff, but require both AliasTable consumers and the issuance adapter to retain/check the full owner key and fail closed if two distinct keys map to one label; add collision vectors. Without that rule, a collision can alias resolution or authorize a certificate for an existing browser origin.

4. **The issuance authorization is field-complete but not byte-complete.** `design.md:64-72` leaves the signed serialization, FQDN normalization, `acme_account` choice (URL or thumbprint), `csr_spki_sha256` encoding, signature envelope, and domain separation open. Because signatures must verify independently at a replaceable adapter, these are interoperability and authorization boundaries, not local implementation details. Pin one canonical encoding and identifiers, with golden, malformed, replay, expiry, wrong-owner, and wrong-CSR vectors.

Once these are amended, the architecture is otherwise ready for implementation: the exact suffix boundary, unchanged `http://hello.mesh`, no shared/wildcard key path, TLS terminator choice, aarch64 measurement, and best-effort WTN adapter are sufficiently pinned.
